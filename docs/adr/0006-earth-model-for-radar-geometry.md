# ADR-0006: Spherical Earth + 4/3 effective-Earth-radius model for radar geometry

Status: Accepted

## Context
S02 (`crates/radar-geo`) needs to map between a radar's polar geometry (azimuth, slant range, elevation angle) and geographic latitude/longitude: great-circle distance/bearing, the destination-point ("direct geodetic") problem, range rings, and beam-height/ground-range approximations, all usable at WSR-88D operational ranges (out to roughly 460 km). `GLOBAL_CONTRACT.md` requires every derived formula to be a standard, citable geodesy/radar-meteorology formula, not something derived ad hoc, and requires derived algorithms to document their assumptions and limitations.

Two families of Earth model are in common use for this kind of work:
- **Ellipsoidal geodesy** (e.g. WGS84, solved via Vincenty's formulae or similar): treats the Earth as an oblate spheroid, correct to sub-millimeter precision at any range, but needs iterative numerical solutions (Vincenty's formulae do not converge in closed form and can fail to converge for near-antipodal points) and meaningfully more implementation and validation effort.
- **Spherical Earth** (a single mean radius): closed-form, simple, well-known formulas (haversine distance, spherical bearing, spherical direct/inverse geodetic problem) with no iteration and no convergence edge cases.

For radar-to-ground beam geometry specifically, operational radar meteorology does not use true Earth curvature at all: it uses the **4/3 effective-Earth-radius model**, which approximates the bending of a radar beam through a normally-refracting atmosphere by pretending the beam travels in a straight line over an Earth enlarged by a factor of 4/3. This is standard practice, not a RadarPro-specific choice (Doviak & Zrnić, *Doppler Radar and Weather Observations*, 2nd ed. (1993), Section 2.2.1).

## Decision
Use a **spherical Earth** model (IUGG mean radius, 6371.0088 km) for all horizontal (latitude/longitude) geometry: great-circle distance, initial bearing, destination point, and range rings. Use the standard **4/3 effective-Earth-radius model** (`effective_earth_radius_km = (4/3) * mean_earth_radius_km`) for beam-height and ground-range/slant-range approximations, per Doviak & Zrnić (1993), Section 2.2.1.

At WSR-88D operational ranges (out to ~460 km), the difference between spherical and WGS84-ellipsoidal horizontal distance/bearing is well under the practical accuracy this stage needs (cursor-to-gate resolution against gates that are hundreds of meters to a few kilometers wide) and is not worth the added implementation/validation complexity of ellipsoidal geodesy. The 4/3-Earth model is not a simplification RadarPro is choosing over a more precise alternative for beam height — it *is* the standard operational model; there is no more-precise "exact" alternative in routine use, since real atmospheric refraction varies with actual atmospheric conditions and full ray-tracing requires input data (a live refractivity profile) this stage does not have.

## Alternatives
- **WGS84 ellipsoidal geodesy (e.g. Vincenty's formulae) for horizontal geometry**: more accurate everywhere, and arguably more "correct" in an absolute sense, but adds iterative numerical solving (with known non-convergence cases for near-antipodal points, irrelevant at radar ranges but still added complexity), a second Earth-shape constant set (semi-major/minor axis, flattening) alongside the mean radius already needed for the 4/3-Earth beam model, and materially more test surface for essentially no benefit at ≤460 km ranges. Rejected for this stage; not rejected forever — see Consequences.
- **Full ray-tracing / variable-refractivity beam-height modeling**: more accurate under non-standard atmospheric conditions (ducting, super-refraction), but requires live atmospheric profile data this stage has no source for, and is a much larger scope than "beam-height utility with documented Earth/refraction assumptions" calls for. Explicitly out of scope per the S02 stage doc.
- **True (non-effective) Earth radius with explicit ray curvature terms for beam height**: mathematically possible but not how operational radar meteorology software is built or validated; would be a bespoke, harder-to-verify derivation instead of the citable standard formula.

## Consequences
**Benefits**: closed-form formulas everywhere (no iterative solvers, no convergence failure modes to guard against); every formula is directly citable to a standard reference (Sinnott 1984 / movable-type.co.uk's standard spherical-trigonometry derivations for distance/bearing/destination point; Doviak & Zrnić 1993 for the 4/3-Earth beam model), satisfying `GLOBAL_CONTRACT.md`'s "no invented behavior" rule; matches how WSR-88D-oriented tools and textbooks already do this class of calculation, making the implementation easy to validate against independently-known values (e.g. ~111.2 km per degree of latitude).

**Costs/risks**: horizontal positions computed here (e.g. range-ring points, resolved cursor-to-gate lookups) carry a small systematic error relative to WGS84-ellipsoidal truth — bounded (well under 1% of distance at these ranges) but nonzero. If a future use case needs sub-meter absolute geographic accuracy (e.g. precise site-survey-grade siting, or cross-referencing against ellipsoidal basemap data at very short range), this decision should be revisited; it is not a permanently settled trade-off, just the right one for this stage's cursor/gate-resolution and rendering needs.

**Scientific implications**: the beam-height approximation is a small-angle approximation on top of the 4/3-Earth model, and both are documented in `crates/radar-geo/src/beam.rs` as degrading in accuracy as elevation angle approaches 90 degrees (never reached by WSR-88D VCPs) and as not accounting for non-standard refraction (ducting/super-refraction). These are documented limitations, not silent gaps.

## Validation
`crates/radar-geo` tests distance/bearing/destination-point round trips at multiple bearings (0°, 90°, 180°, 270°, and an oblique angle) and distances (short ~1 km, WSR-88D-typical ~100 km, near-max-range ~460 km), plus an independently-known reference value (~111.2 km per degree of latitude along a meridian, and the quarter-Earth-circumference distance along the equator). Beam-height/ground-range tests check the expected monotonic direction of change with increasing elevation angle at KTLX-scale ranges (up to ~460 km) and confirm no panics as elevation approaches ±90 degrees. See `crates/radar-geo/src/spherical.rs` and `crates/radar-geo/src/beam.rs` test modules.
